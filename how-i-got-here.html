How I got here

Why I became interested in the intersection of brain research and education. It stems from six years of playing baseball.

The worst player on the team

I was the only person in my baseball club who had started playing in middle school. Everyone else had been playing since they were six or seven. That difference is not small, and it does not close quickly — I was the worst player on the team, and it was obvious to everyone, including me.

Our coach was frightening, and I was the one who got shouted at. I had joined because I wanted to play. Within a few months I had stopped wanting anything at all. I kept turning up, but only because leaving would have been worse.

What the lockdown gave back

Then COVID cancelled practice. My first reaction was relief — I finally did not have to go.

But with the schedule gone, and nothing to fill it with, I ended up watching professional baseball for hours a day. Not out of duty. Because I wanted to. That was when I realised I still liked this sport, and that I had joined for one reason: I wanted to get good at it. Being bad had made me forget that.

I bought a bat and started practising on my own. From that point until I finished high school baseball I did not skip a day — at least two hours, two hundred swings, every night.

The body that would not follow instructions

I did get better. But not nearly as much better as the hours should have bought me. And the way I failed was strange enough that I could not stop thinking about it.

A coach would give a teammate an instruction, and the teammate would simply do it — the correction appeared in their body almost immediately. I would receive the same instruction, execute what I was certain was the same correction, and then watch the video. My body had not done what I told it to do, and it had not felt like anything was wrong.

That is a strange experience to have about yourself. It is not laziness and it is not misunderstanding the instruction — it is a failure of translation between an intention and a movement, and you cannot detect it from the inside. Later I learned that researchers study exactly this, and that when a motor skill is first acquired appears to matter a great deal. Starting at twelve is not the same as starting at six.

That is where the brain stopped being a school subject for me. Not a class, not a book — a video of myself failing to do what I was sure I was doing.

Two things at once

I was never the reason we won. But I was on the team the year we reached the metropolitan tournament — the school's best result in five years.

Meanwhile there was the other problem: baseball had taken all of my time, and I still intended to do well academically. I could not afford to study inefficiently, because there were not enough hours left to be inefficient with. So I did the only thing available and started reading — books and papers on how learning actually works, then rebuilding how I studied around what I found.

The difference was not subtle. Same person, roughly the same hours, a completely different return. I finished 2nd out of 400 students.

Two experiments, run at the same time on the same person. In one, effort alone did not transfer into the movement I wanted. In the other, changing the method changed the result almost immediately. I had not become smarter. I had changed how I learned.

What follows from that

I only found that method because a scheduling problem forced me to look for one. Nobody taught it to me; it was not part of school. Which means most students never have that accident, and they are not less capable — they are simply working with a method nobody handed them.

If method makes that much difference, then access to a good method is itself a form of inequality — and it is invisible, because from the outside it looks like talent.

A school trip to the United States made that concrete for me. Not because American education is better — it is different in ways I am still working out — but because seeing another system up close made the one I grew up inside look like a choice rather than a fact. Travelling in Cambodia pushed the same point much further. What a person ends up able to do depends enormously on where they happened to be taught, and almost none of that is about them.

What I do about it

I chose Cognitive and Behavioral Neuroscience with a minor in Education Studies for a specific reason: the science on one side, the practice on the other. The people who know what is true about learning and the people who decide what happens in a classroom are largely different people, and they are not in the same room often enough.

So I try to keep a foot in both. Research work has taught me how slow and careful real evidence is — how much of it is setting up a camera correctly and labelling video honestly, long before anything gets claimed. Teaching has taught me the opposite lesson: a method that is correct but unteachable is worth nothing, and most of the distance between research and practice is not disagreement but translation.

Helping Japanese students find a route to studying abroad is the same problem in another form. The route exists. The scholarships exist. What is missing is that almost nobody is told they are there — which, again, ends up looking like ability.

Where and when I've done these things is on the portfolio page; this one is about why.

Where I'd like to end up

My interest sits at the intersection of cognitive science and education. What I want to do is apply research on the brain to how people are actually taught, and use it to raise the quality of education — not for one classroom, but as broadly as I can reach. Narrowing the gap in who gets access to a good education is the specific version of that I care about most.

More broadly, I'm interested in applying what we learn about the brain to society in general. Education is the first place I want to take it, because it is where the leverage is largest and where I have felt the difference myself. It is not where I intend to stop.

Concretely, that means psychology, neuroscience, developmental research, education, and education technology — which sounds like five fields, but from where I stand it is one question approached from five directions.
